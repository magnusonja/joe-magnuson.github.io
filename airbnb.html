Business Intelligence Case Study

Airbnb Market Demand Analysis

Google Sheets · October 2025

Finding attractive opportunities in the Manhattan vacation-rental market

An early analysis of NYC Airbnb listings focused on identifying which neighborhoods and property sizes appeared most attractive for vacation rentals, then estimating the revenue potential of listings that matched those characteristics.

The business questions

The analysis was designed to answer three practical questions:

  1. Which neighborhoods are most attractive for vacation rentals?
  2. Which property sizes are most popular?
  3. Do different neighborhoods favor different property sizes, and what revenue potential do listings matching those characteristics show?

Because direct occupancy data was not available, the project used reviews from the previous 12 months as a proxy for rental frequency and attractiveness.

Preparing the data

Data preparation was an important part of the analysis.

I created cleaned versions of the neighborhood and bedroom fields rather than modifying the raw data directly. Neighborhood labels were standardized to address inconsistent capitalization and trailing spaces, while blank bedroom values were converted to zero to represent studio listings.

I also prepared the calendar data for revenue analysis by calculating revenue earned for rented nights and bringing those values back into the listings data.

This created a cleaner foundation for the comparisons that followed.

Property size patterns

The project first examined property size by number of bedrooms to understand which listing types were most common in the market.

Listing counts

1-bedroom listings led the market

1-bedroom: 1,265 listings · 2-bedroom: 526 listings · Studio: 441 listings

The analysis showed that smaller property sizes, particularly 1-bedroom listings and studios, were important segments of the market.

This established the property-size patterns that would be considered alongside neighborhood attractiveness.

Neighborhood attractiveness

To identify the most attractive neighborhoods, I used reviews from the previous 12 months as a proxy for rental activity.

The top 10 neighborhoods were:

  1. Lower East Side
  2. Hell's Kitchen
  3. Harlem
  4. Midtown
  5. Upper West Side
  6. Chelsea
  7. East Village
  8. East Harlem
  9. West Village
  10. Upper East Side

Lower East Side, Hell's Kitchen, and Harlem ranked highest on the project's rental-activity measure. These neighborhoods became the focus of the next stage of analysis.

Neighborhood + property size fit

The next step was to determine whether the most popular property size varied across the top neighborhoods.

The analysis showed that 1-bedroom listings were the most popular size across the top neighborhoods except Midtown, where studios were the leading property size.

This created a straightforward set of neighborhood and property-size criteria for the revenue analysis. Rather than evaluating every listing equally, I narrowed the dataset to listings that matched the identified characteristics.

Revenue potential

After identifying the target neighborhoods and property sizes, I flagged listings that matched those criteria and used the calendar data to estimate revenue.

Revenue was calculated from the nightly adjusted price for rented nights during the 30-day sample period. The project then annualized that 30-day result by multiplying it by 12.

Highest-earning listing

Listing 49946551 · $29,940

The listing generated $29,940 during the 30-day sample period. That corresponds to an estimated annual revenue of $359,280 if the same monthly revenue level were maintained throughout the year.

What the analysis showed

The analysis pointed to a relatively concentrated set of opportunities within the Manhattan market examined.

  • Lower East Side, Hell's Kitchen, and Harlem ranked highest on the project's rental-activity measure.
  • 1-bedroom listings were the most common property size, followed by 2-bedroom listings and studios.
  • Property-size preferences were not identical across neighborhoods, with Midtown favoring studios while the other top neighborhoods favored 1-bedroom listings.
  • After applying those neighborhood and property-size criteria, the analysis identified individual listings with substantial revenue during the sample period.

The result was a descriptive analysis of the available data, not a prediction of future performance.

What I took from the project

This was one of my early projects working seriously with data preparation and business-oriented analysis.

The work taught me that the quality of the answer depends heavily on the structure of the analysis that comes before it: clean the data, define the measure being used, narrow the population, and then compare the results.

It also gave me an early opportunity to turn spreadsheet analysis into a business recommendation rather than stopping at a collection of pivot tables.

That foundation carried forward into later projects, where the questions became more complex and the analysis moved beyond descriptive comparisons into investigating relationships between variables and communicating their limitations.
